Screen Recording Legal Guide: What You Can and Can't Record

✍️ Waqar Ahmed 📅 September 2026 ⏱️ 8 min read

Screen recording is a powerful tool, but it exists within a legal framework. Understanding what you can and cannot legally record is essential — both to protect yourself and to respect others' rights.

In this guide, I'll explain the legal landscape of screen recording in plain language. This is educational content, not legal advice — for specific situations, consult a qualified attorney in your jurisdiction.

⚠️ Disclaimer: This article provides general information about screen recording laws. Laws vary by country and situation. Consult a legal professional for advice specific to your circumstances.

General Rule: Recording Your Own Screen is Legal

Recording your own screen — your computer, your activities, your creations — is legal in virtually every jurisdiction. Your screen is your property, and you have every right to capture what appears on it.

Problems arise when your recording captures other people, copyrighted content, or private information.

Copyright: The Big Issue

Copyright law protects creative works — movies, TV shows, music, books, video games, software, and more. Recording copyrighted content without permission may violate these laws.

Generally Safe to Record (Your Content)

  • Your own screen activity
  • Your own videos and creations
  • Public domain content (very old movies, classic literature, etc.)
  • Content with Creative Commons licenses (check specific license)
  • Your own gameplay in games you legally own

Usually NOT Safe to Record

  • Netflix, Disney+, Amazon Prime, Hulu content
  • Paid online courses (Udemy, Coursera, etc.)
  • Copyrighted music videos
  • Live sports broadcasts (NFL, NBA, Premier League)
  • Commercial software demonstrations
  • YouTube videos (without creator's permission)
  • Other people's social media content

Fair Use and Screen Recording

"Fair use" is a legal doctrine that allows limited use of copyrighted material without permission. It's often misunderstood — fair use is a defense, not a right.

Four factors determine fair use:

  1. Purpose: Educational, commentary, or transformative use weighs in favor
  2. Nature: Factual content weighs in favor; creative content weighs against
  3. Amount: Using small portions weighs in favor; using the whole work weighs against
  4. Effect: If your use harms the original's market, it weighs against
💡 Fair Use Examples: Recording a few seconds of a movie to review it, capturing gameplay for a commentary video, or using short clips in educational content. These are often fair use — but not guaranteed.

Consent: Recording Other People

Different jurisdictions have different rules about recording conversations and meetings:

One-Party Consent (Most US States)

Only one person in the conversation needs to consent. If you're part of the conversation, you can record it.

All-Party Consent (Some US States, EU)

Everyone in the conversation must consent. This includes California, Florida, and most EU countries.

Best Practice

Always inform participants before recording, regardless of your local laws. It's ethical, builds trust, and avoids legal issues.

Recording Online Meetings

Zoom Meetings

Zoom has a built-in recording feature that shows a notification to all participants. For third-party recorders, you should inform participants verbally. Zoom's terms of service allow recording with consent.

Google Meet

Similar to Zoom — inform participants before recording. Google's terms allow recording with everyone's consent.

Microsoft Teams

Teams allows recording with consent. Notifications appear for participants.

Best Practices for Meetings

  • Announce at the start: "This meeting is being recorded"
  • Get verbal or written consent from all participants
  • If anyone objects, don't record
  • Store recordings securely
  • Don't share recordings without permission

Recording Online Classes

Online classes are a gray area:

  • Student's perspective: Recording lectures for personal study is often acceptable if the institution permits it
  • Teacher's perspective: Teachers can record their own lectures
  • Institutional policy: Many schools have explicit rules about recording
  • Best practice: Always ask permission first

Recording Video Games

Game recording is generally more permissive than other media:

  • Most games allow recording for personal use and Let's Play videos
  • Some games have restrictions — check the EULA (End User License Agreement)
  • Nintendo has historically been strict about gameplay videos
  • Monetization may require additional permissions
  • Fair use typically protects commentary, reviews, and tutorials

Recording Software and Applications

Recording software you legally own is generally fine. But recording tutorials for commercial software may require permission:

  • Your own work in software: Fine to record
  • Tutorials about software: Usually fair use if transformative
  • Demonstrating paid software: Check EULA
  • Recording competitors' software: Legal but be careful with claims

Recording YouTube Videos

Recording YouTube videos without permission raises copyright concerns:

  • Personal offline viewing: Generally tolerated
  • Re-uploading: Illegal without permission
  • Commentary/criticism: Often fair use
  • Educational use: Sometimes fair use — depends on context
  • Monetizing someone's content: Usually illegal

Recording Live Streams

Live streams are copyrighted content. Recording them without permission may violate copyright law and platform terms:

  • Twitch streams: Copyrighted by streamer
  • YouTube Live: Same rules as regular YouTube videos
  • Sports broadcasts: Highly protected
  • Your own streams: Fine to record

Privacy Concerns

Screen recordings often capture personal information:

  • Emails: Hide inbox before recording
  • Passwords: Never show credentials on screen
  • Financial info: Close banking tabs
  • Private messages: Close chat apps
  • Personal photos: Hide desktop icons

GDPR and Data Protection

If you're in the EU or recording EU citizens, GDPR applies:

  • Consent required: For recording personal data
  • Purpose limitation: Use recordings only for stated purpose
  • Storage limits: Don't keep recordings longer than necessary
  • Right to erasure: People can request deletion
  • Security: Protect recordings from unauthorized access

Best Practices — Always Safe Approach

  • Get permission: When in doubt, ask first
  • Inform participants: Always announce recordings
  • Hide sensitive info: Clean your screen before recording
  • Use for personal purposes: Don't distribute without permission
  • Attribute creators: Give credit where due
  • Follow platform rules: YouTube, Zoom, etc. have their own terms
  • Consult a lawyer: For commercial or complex situations
